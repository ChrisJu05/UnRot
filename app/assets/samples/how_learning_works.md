# How Learning Works

## The Forgetting Curve

In the 1880s the psychologist **Hermann Ebbinghaus** memorised lists of nonsense syllables and tested himself at different intervals. He found that memory decays quickly at first and then more slowly: much of what we learn is forgotten within a day unless it is reviewed. This pattern is called the forgetting curve. A simple model of it is R(t) = e^(-t/S), where R is the share of information you still remember, t is the time in days and S is the stability of the memory. Each time a memory is successfully retrieved, the curve becomes flatter, which means the information is forgotten more slowly afterwards.

## Spaced Repetition

Spaced repetition uses the forgetting curve to schedule reviews. Instead of cramming, material is reviewed at increasing intervals, for example after one day, three days, a week and a month. Reviewing just before something would be forgotten makes the memory more durable. Software such as flashcard apps often uses the SM-2 algorithm, which adjusts the interval for each card based on how easy the learner found it. Cards that are answered correctly move further apart, while forgotten cards come back soon.

## Active Recall

Active recall means retrieving information from memory instead of passively re-reading it. Answering questions, explaining a concept in your own words or writing down everything you remember are all forms of active recall. Research on the **testing effect** shows that practising retrieval leads to better long-term retention than spending the same time re-reading. Retrieval is effortful, and this desirable difficulty is exactly what strengthens memory.

## Interleaving and Elaboration

Interleaving mixes different topics or problem types within one study session instead of practising one type in a block. It feels harder, but it helps learners distinguish between concepts and choose the right strategy. Elaboration connects new information to what you already know, for example by asking why something is true or by finding concrete examples. Dual coding combines words with visuals such as sketches and diagrams, giving the brain two ways to retrieve the same idea.

## Sleep and Memory Consolidation

During sleep, the brain consolidates memories: the hippocampus replays recent experiences and helps transfer them into long-term storage in the neocortex. Students who sleep after learning remember more than those who stay awake for the same amount of time. Short breaks between study sessions also support consolidation, which is one reason why short, focused learning bursts can be very effective.

## Cognitive Load

Working memory can only hold a few pieces of information at the same time. Cognitive load theory distinguishes intrinsic load, which comes from the complexity of the material itself, from extraneous load, which is caused by poor presentation such as cluttered slides. Breaking material into small chunks and presenting one idea at a time reduces extraneous load, so that more working memory is available for actually understanding the content.
